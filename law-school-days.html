11/16/2021

I got up at six in the morning, put on blond Eckbert in German, and then drifted back to sleep.

I dreamed that several classmates and I were hiking toward Tibet. The mountains on both sides of the national highway seemed as though they might press down on us. There was almost no vegetation.

We passed through a vegetable market. I wandered around inside, and when I came out, my classmates had already gone on without me. I was alone again.

It was getting dark, so I thought I had to find somewhere to stay. I stopped at a random house and asked whether I could spend the night. Then it started to rain. I could hear the rain tapping all around the room, and water was splashing in through the gaps.

I opened a glass door and discovered that my classmates were staying in a hotel just across a patch of grass. One of them was MH. He ran over, saying, “You’re here!” I immediately started worrying about how to tell the kind person who had taken me in that I wanted to go stay with my classmates. MH slipped and got his clothes wet.

Later I dreamed that several of us were sleeping side by side, like family. Both sides of my arms felt warm. A classmate beside me was reading a diary aloud. There were simple black line drawings on the page.

I listened carefully and thought: so this classmate is speaking German, and speaking it so well. The pronunciation was so standard. Suddenly German felt like such a warm language.

Then I woke up. It was my phone reading German aloud. (Laughs.) The blanket really was warm, though. Listening to a classmate read German beside me felt very comforting.

10/28/2021

When we ran a guesthouse on Gulangyu, Maizi built a glass room on the rooftop. At first he made it from the kind of old windows that became common after Liberation; we had picked them up together from a junkyard. Later the leaking became a serious problem, so he decided to use treated wood with mortise-and-tenon joints, cover it with tempered glass, and fill the gaps with silicone. That could solve the leaking.

I suddenly remembered this because my room now gets too much light and feels very much like that glass room. I remember that only quite late did we decide to buy curtains, which solved the problem of the room being uninhabitable during the day.

How many choices we made in the past were really the result of free will? If it were truly free will, I would rather that room had simply been a glass conservatory with a sofa, somewhere to lounge around and play games. But once it became a guesthouse, making that choice meant giving up a considerable amount of income.

I started wondering when, exactly, I had completely forgotten what I wanted in the beginning. Maizi, on the other hand, kept doing the things he had originally wanted to do when he chose to stay on Gulangyu, and it seems the enjoyment was still there.

Looking back, although I thought his decision was somewhat irresponsible at the time, perhaps only by continuing to remain free and light could all the hard labor of running the guesthouse avoid becoming meaningless.

What I can see from past experience is that my most basic requirement for myself should be: stay awake to life, and stay awake to myself.

For example, do not forget to feel carefully those moments when the sunlight is gentle and the green trees across the little river glitter like fish scales.

Life has to have these things first. Otherwise, all the other effort is wasted.

10/18/2021

I hadn’t smoked Seven Wolves cigarettes in many years.

In 2012, I hitchhiked from Chaozhou to Xiamen, flagging down rides along the national highway. The driver offered me a Seven Wolves. It had a faint sweetness in the aftertaste, followed by a monotonous taste like burning paper. I didn’t smoke much then and thought it was pretty strong.

Halfway there we passed Yunxiao and stopped to buy loquats. The woman selling them wore a straw hat with a piece of floral cloth around the brim to protect her from the sun. She was embarrassed about having her photo taken, but also really liked being photographed. Her husband stood nearby and told her not to bother me.

She asked me to advertise her loquats for her. She came up with a slogan herself. I vaguely remember that it began with “Yunxiao loquats.” Along the national highway, the orchards stretched out in large patches, and the sign at her place simply read “Yunxiao Loquats.”

The truck driver was a round-faced middle-aged man with sad eyes. Before I got out, he told me that someday he wanted to live like a herdsman and raise a few sheep.

When we reached Xiamen, it was drizzling and the road was muddy. He saw a bus and told me I could take it to the ferry and cross by boat. Other cars were honking behind us. He watched as I got out.

All of this was almost ten years ago now. I wonder whether his wish ever came true.

10/09/2021

Reading on the balcony, I suddenly thought that H understands expression better than she does, even though she is the one who talks endlessly about herself.

She has all kinds of assumptions, a toolbox of behaviors ready to be used at any moment. These tools have been put together for all kinds of reasons. They come from romance novels, television dramas, and things heard from other people. In those plots, there is a vague causal relationship between certain behaviors and certain results.

This makes some of her love manipulative. Compared with love itself, using these tools and watching the reactions they produce in the person she loves seems to fascinate her more. Meaningless behaviors are then woven into the relationship.

Her love begins to look like a clumsy performance. It also contradicts the justice she claims to care about: before putting on a good play, capturing another person’s heart is necessary in order to obtain a qualified actor, but this actor must never know that he is performing.

Endless tests keep appearing: if he loves me, then when I do this, he “should” respond like that. Why didn’t he respond like that?

I remember very clearly that at a gathering with my father and some neighbors, she began to dance. They kept cheering. Gradually she became attracted to those sounds and kept making more exaggerated movements to win more applause.

For many years, she often felt very sad that, if not for poverty, perhaps she could have continued developing this talent. She was certain the talent existed because the applause had confirmed it.

The dancing itself was forgotten.

In another way, this shows that for other people’s recognition, she was willing to endure almost anything. She could even tailor a whole series of behaviors to obtain recognition.

The ability to endure hardship is, after all, a strength. It is just that these outward behaviors themselves are meaningless.

When thoughts remain unspoken, you do not realize how wrong you are.

Does that mean that only what has been expressed can really be called correct understanding?

This is another one of her habits: learning lessons from failure, collecting things that seem to have an exact cause and effect.

But life is not a perfectly tuned keyboard. The note may be wrong, while the score — the rule — may be completely correct. It is just that the rule can produce that result only under ideal conditions.

Sometimes failure is simply failure. You do not necessarily become wiser from every failure.

(But that, too, is another rule.)

There was nothing extraordinary about this. It was just that I was standing outside the train station with everything I owned, like an arrow already on the string, with no choice but to be released.

I was charging my phone with a power bank. The labor law elective I had signed up for was about to begin. At the same time, I kept refreshing train tickets and hostel listings.

There was nowhere I could go.

So, still like an arrow on the string, I returned to Quanzhou, the place I had left only a few days earlier with everything I owned.

I found somewhere to stay in a village. Along the river, instead of people sitting around chatting and cooling off, there were women in black dresses standing at the mouths of the alleys, one every few lanes.

The hostel was empty. Dust clung to the coral-fleece sofa covers. Things were scattered around. The whole place had a run-down look. I soon left again.

That morning I refreshed the system several times, but my COVID test result still had not appeared. I called the hospital. They said the electronic version would not be available for another two days.

Two days meant forty-eight hours. Applying to return to campus one day in advance meant seventy-two.

I could not go back to school. I could have waited at home, but I was sitting outside the train station as the sky grew dark, with a bag beside me packed full of things: a speaker, a bowl, a toothbrush, a kettle, and all kinds of miscellaneous things crammed together.

08/13/2021

Today I came back alone from the high-speed rail station, and I still couldn’t stop myself from crying.

For the past month, my daughter and I had walked this road together every day.

The other thing is that this kind of drifting means the memories you build anywhere are always temporary. Once my daughter and I leave this place, everything here will have nothing to do with us anymore. Little by little, it will all be forgotten.

This is our life. When I think about it, it feels cruel.

My daughter remembers moving twice. The first time was leaving the “three little cats house” on Gulangyu. The second was leaving the “slide house” in Jiaomei.

I remember the day we left Gulangyu. She had taken an unusually good nap and did not wake up until evening, when it was almost dark. I hung her potty and a few toys we could take with us from the stroller. Everything else stayed behind.

Leaving happened just like that, without my ever really explaining it to her.

Leaving Jiaomei was even more sudden. Because of the arguments around the divorce, her father simply took her away.

During the Mid-Autumn Festival in 2018, I did not know where my daughter was. I knew that even if I wanted to see her, I probably would not be able to. So I did not struggle.

In Jiaomei, everything I looked at reminded me of her, much as it does now, so I left there very soon too.

Later, my daughter went back to Jiaomei once. Neither she nor we knew that the apartment would actually be sold, so in a sense she left that place vaguely too, without ever quite knowing it was goodbye.

This afternoon, after dinner, she put on her backpack and started crying.

At the time, I thought it was all right. Quanzhou is right next door. If we want to see each other, we can.

But now I am crying too.

Every place you have lived in becomes tied to the memories of ordinary days spent there. It is difficult not to grow attached. Leaving is rarely painless.

If this were not bound up with the fact that our family had, in some sense, already come apart, perhaps it would not feel quite this sad.

I am crying now partly because my heart aches for my daughter.

Every time I go back to see her, I stay at the same guesthouse. The family who runs it is very kind, and by now we know each other well. But every time I leave, I have to empty the room all over again.

My daughter laughs at me: “Mom, every time you come here, are you moving again?”

Original 中文

今天从高铁站一个人回来，还是忍不住泪奔了。一个月来天天和孩子两个人走这条路。另一个就是，这种漂泊就是在任何地方构建的回忆都是短期的，想想和孩子一离开这里，一切就都与我们无关了，渐渐的也会被遗忘。这就是我们的生活，想想挺残酷的。小孩记得她搬过两次家，一次是离开“三只小猫家”（鼓浪屿），第二次是离开“滑滑梯家”（角美）。记得离开鼓浪屿那一天，她午睡睡得特别好，到傍晚天快黑的时候才睡醒。我在婴儿车里吗挂了她的马桶和一些能带走的玩具，其他的都留在那里了。离开就这样也没有和她解释过就发生了。离开角美更突然了，因为离婚的事情吵架，他爸爸干脆带走了她。2018年的中秋节，我不知道孩子在哪里，想见肯定也是见不到的。于是我没有挣扎。因为在角美每天睹物思人（像我现在这样），于是我也很快离开了。后来孩子有回去角美一次，她和我们都不知道房子会真的卖掉，所以等于也是模模糊糊地离开。

今天下午吃完晚饭，她背好背包的时候哭了。我那时候觉得还好，泉州就在隔壁，相见就能见嘛。但是我现在也哭了。确实，每一个住过的地方，很难不倾注感情，和朝夕相处的回忆联系在一起，离开都很难不伤筋动骨的。如果不是和家已经在某种程度上散了这件事情结合在一起，估计也不会像现在这样悲哀吧。

我现在哭，也是心疼孩子。每次我回去看她，都是住在同一家民宿，民宿一家人非常友善，已经很熟了。但是每次离开，都要清空一次房间。孩子笑我，“妈妈你每次来都是搬家吗？”
