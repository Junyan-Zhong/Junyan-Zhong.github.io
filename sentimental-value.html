Sentimental Value

On abandonment, being chosen, and the limits of forgiveness.

The director seems to place the answer in forgiveness — a very old, obvious, but wrong answer. He seems to sense Nora’s pain but does not truly understand its inner workings, so he designs a reconciliation that pulls her back into it, mistakenly believing it achieves some kind of wholeness.

I remember listening to a talk by Justine Musk where she mentioned that Musk often says “No.” In the film, the director and father, Gustav, is talking with the popular actress Rachel on the beach. Rachel says she wants to zone out for a while, but every day she is entangled in too many things. Gustav says, “Just say no.” From this, you can glimpse something essential about his personality. He completely left his wife. He said no.

We do not know whether he disappeared without a trace or came back occasionally. From the film, it seems his daughters only saw him a few times over several decades. Saying no is not itself a bad thing. Leaving a quarrelsome, unhappy marriage is not necessarily anyone’s fault. Sometimes two people are simply incompatible, and they can and should leave each other.

But Gustav’s way of leaving was to vanish.

The mother did something similar in another direction. She did not even transfer ownership of the house for years, as if she lacked the motivation to straighten out her life. Is this kind of love really a good thing? Why, when love is gone, does what remains become endless hatred? Perhaps it is because I have never experienced that kind of bone-deep hatred. Or maybe it is because I have never experienced that kind of love.

Nora and her sister are forced to grow up with an absent father, facing alone a mother who has broken down and can no longer care for them. Nora takes on a maternal role, washing her sister’s hair, combing it, sending her to school. I do not know how many people were moved by this scene. It reminded me of a scene in Problem Restaurant, where a divorced mother cannot care for her young daughter, and Chika stands on a little stool, replaying a cooking tutorial while washing rice with her tiny hands and measuring the water with her palm.

The younger sister, Agnes, has a steady personality. Her marriage is calm, perhaps so calm that it barely looks happy from the outside. She does not smile much. It made me think again about what happiness is. Maybe happiness is not joy but peace. If there is peace in a marriage, perhaps that is already something.

Nora sees her sister as happy while judging herself harshly. In bed with a married actor she works with, she says it is good that he is married because then he will not see how “fucked up” she really is. Nora has an extremely low opinion of herself. She carries the marks of someone who was never fully affirmed or seen by her father.

Later, when she learns that the actor has divorced, she tries to kiss him, but he refuses. She is confused. His hesitation seems to trigger something much older than the relationship itself. For several days afterward, she withdraws into gloom, almost as though she has been abandoned by her father again. Who the actor is matters less than the feeling he activates. The abandonment is more real than the affection.

In Nora’s inner world, she seems condemned to push the same boulder uphill again and again: the psychological weight left by her father, her lack of confidence, the wound of being abandoned. With whatever strength she has left, she lives for her artistic life, but fear keeps cutting through it.

The most tragic moment comes before her performance on stage. It feels like a final struggle before death. Then she walks toward it anyway. After the performance, when she bows, you can faintly see black tape holding together the torn seams of her dress. Behind the ordinary appearance is a soul that has been repeatedly torn and repaired.

Nora tells her sister, “You turned out fine, and I’m so fucked up.” Her sister answers that she turned out fine because she had Nora — because when their mother fell apart, Nora washed her hair, combed it, and took her to school.

But the film never really arrives at a rational moment of symbolic separation from the father. Both sisters still love him deeply. Even though he wounded them, they remain hopeful about him. The father, once heroic and now quietly aged, has such a lonely gaze that even the audience can be moved by him, let alone his daughters.

He remains absorbed in himself: the light and shadow of the old house, the sunset, tree shadows moving. He wants Nora to make a film about his own mother, who one day decided to die. Nora refuses. Later, he leaves her a message, like a prodigal man returning, saying he wants to talk properly.

In many ways, a father can become a template for a romantic partner. Gustav wants to fulfill his own desire to make the film, while Nora’s childhood remains an open wound. The flame of her hope for her father’s love has not gone out even after more than twenty years.

When he tells her, “I see my own reflection in you,” her heart seems to tremble. She feels an unfamiliar happiness mixed with uncertainty, afraid he may only be saying it casually. But his apparent understanding is still filtered through himself. He sees his own loss of his mother and projects it onto his daughters’ loss of him. That is not the same as truly understanding them.

At the grandson’s birthday party, the daughters long for a real conversation or reconciliation, but speaking to him feels like punching cotton. His evasive words contain some truth, but he seems to forget that much of this pain was created by his own disappearance.

Both sisters want their father’s attention. Agnes once acted in one of his films. Years later, he wants her son to play a role. The usually calm Agnes finally explains what that experience did to her: while she was acting, she had all of her father’s attention. It felt like the center of the universe. Then his attention vanished. What followed was endless waiting for it to return.

That may be the most painful part.

Nora is the same. In Gustav’s script, he briefly mentions his mother’s decision to die. Nora reads this as if her father has finally seen her struggle, as if he is offering compassion from a great distance. The connection is incredibly thin, yet even that is enough to make her feel almost seen, as though years of grievance might finally dissolve.

This is where I disagree most strongly with the film. It seems to place its answer in forgiveness. But with a father who can repeatedly disappear and cannot provide sustained attention, even without aggression or overt violence, a daughter’s emotions can be thrown up and down endlessly. As long as she holds even the slightest hope that he might change, the wound can replay again and again.

Giving sustained love is a capability. Facing death does not automatically teach a selfish person how to love. The weakness of this transformation lies in the unspoken sentence: “After all, he is still your father.”

It is not surprising that Nora falls in love with men who resemble him, repeatedly forgiving what hurts her.

Rachel provides a contrast. She cannot fully enter Gustav’s trauma-laden language, and when he tries to alter her subjectivity, she notices immediately. She asks why he wanted her to dye her hair the same color as his daughter’s.

Rachel is independent and internally coherent. During rehearsal, when the actress playing the mother delivers a bleak line, Rachel responds not with silent despair but with emotional release. She embraces the actress and later says, “I wish she could have gotten a hug like that back then.”

The director seems almost awkward about this response, because the protagonist he needs must possess a sharper, more inward sorrow. Later Rachel tells Nora, “I can’t be like you,” and cries. But not being like Nora — not fitting the temperament of someone drawn toward self-destruction — is not a failure. It may be a kind of luck.

A traumatized daughter longs to be chosen, but often overlooks what the father is actually choosing. Being chosen does not mean the trauma has been seen.

Rachel offers another answer: not clinging to being chosen, but turning away when something feels wrong.
