Sunday in the Park with George

On Georges Seurat, Sondheim, concentration, and the art of making art.

This is probably the third artist I’ve come across this year who died in his thirties. Georges Seurat (1859–1891) died at just 31. His masterpiece, A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte, is now at the Art Institute of Chicago.

The musical imagines how this painter created that painting in 1884–1886. I had never really thought of a painter as such a long-term thinker, working with so much concentration and discipline. Sondheim uses Seurat to ask what the “art of making art” actually means.

Seurat’s pointillist technique is built from separate dots of color that mix in the eye. Blue and red can remain separate on the canvas while becoming purple in perception. That process becomes a metaphor for artistic work itself: bit by bit, decision by decision.

When an artist tries to do something new, harsh criticism is almost inevitable. The artist has to decide whether to follow approval or vision. One of the things I love about George is his refusal to make other people’s approval the measure of his work.

My friend once went to Chicago just to see this painting in person, so when I learned that Sondheim had written a musical around it, I was completely fascinated. It felt as if several dots in my own life suddenly connected.

I watched the full musical and found Bernadette Peters extraordinary. What moved me most was how much the work says about concentration. At first, I thought concentration meant simply being still. But I came to understand it as something larger: being where you are, rather than living in the past or the future.

I worry too much about tomorrow. I have often imagined that life could be made perfect if I thought hard enough about it. This musical reminded me that attention belongs to the present.

Notice every tree. Understand the light. Concentrate on now.

Another idea stayed with me: stop worrying about whether your vision is new. Let other people decide that. Keep moving on.

Art is not easy. Every minor detail becomes a major decision. You have to keep things in scale and hold to your vision. The work is assembled gradually, with time, craft, perseverance, and a little luck.

That may be what I love most about Sondheim’s idea of art: inspiration matters, but craft is what gives inspiration a form.

Originally written after watching the 1986 filmed production.
