04/01/2022

Duidui’s Grandfather’s Funeral, When Everything Can No Longer Be Undone

I have been living here for almost a month. Last week Duidui started taking classes online. There was so much homework that it became hard to keep track of, so we decided that during the day she would go to Maizi’s place to do her work. When I have no class, I go downstairs and sit for a while on a stone bench, smoke, and read. My state of mind goes back and forth, but I can open Reading and Taking Pleasure in Life’s Ups and Downs at any time and keep reading.

By the third cigarette I usually feel cold, and sleepy too. I just want to finish quickly and go back upstairs. There is an emptiness inside me. Ma Yan writes about going on outings with friends, eating roast duck, going to bookstores, while I am here in the middle of a vegetable market sealed off during the pandemic, surrounded by six- and seven-story buildings. When there is no sun, the place where I sit is very cold. When there is sun, I look up from my book and everything around me seems covered in green. I have no friends and no connections in this city. It has been this way for a long time.

Life goes on as usual.

There is a shop in the alley that sells things for funerals, bundles and bundles of gold-paper ingots. At night the shop is dim. Several people from the shop sit outside watching pedestrians go through the alley, beneath a low awning. I told Duidui that these are the only things we can buy for Grandpa now. We cannot buy him anything else anymore. Duidui thought for a moment, her little eyes above her mask looking straight ahead. “We can still buy things for Great-Grandma.” I was startled. Had something happened to Great-Grandma? She corrected herself: we could still buy Great-Grandma other presents. “But I don’t know her birthday.” If you mean it, you don’t need a birthday, I said, rather deliberately. When I got married, Great-Grandma gave me a blue gemstone she called the “Heart of the Ocean.” I really have never bought her a gift.

We live on the sixth floor, and neither Midui nor I likes climbing the stairs. The smell of paint in the stairwell is strong. Usually I just keep climbing until my legs ache terribly. There is a light on the seventh floor that is always on. Its dull yellow light falls on the wall at the half-landing above the fifth floor, and that is how I know I am almost at the sixth.

A strip of red cloth was tied at the doorway. One morning in early March, Duidui’s grandfather went out to buy vegetables, and we never saw him again. On the day of the funeral, we saw him for the last time at the morgue of No. 95 Hospital. A security guard pointed us toward the morgue. The road was wide. The spring sunlight was mild and quiet. The trees were sparse, and the light made everything look as though it were covered by a thin mist. The guard said the place had heavy yin energy and that perhaps a child should not go there. Grandpa was there, so I thought it should be all right.

The morgue was a one-story building. There was a platform with offerings on it. Maizi was burning paper ingots, and the air smelled of burning paper. Maizi’s grandfather, second aunt, aunt, and uncle stood by the roadside in black clothes. Behind them was a low wall with weeds growing on it, and beyond that a small patch of private farmland. Relatives came over and touched Midui’s head. She was wearing a mask. In a notebook were the words “Grandpa, follow along,” something to be recited when the spirit car crossed a bridge or an intersection.

There was a black puppy at the entrance to the morgue, with a round face, climbing up and down the dirty stairs. Duidui stood outside the room where the body was kept, watching the puppy and saying, “So cute.” A few years ago, at my niece Hanhan’s grandmother’s funeral, Hanhan played with the rice placed beside her grandmother’s coffin. At that moment I was simply numb. I did not know what any of it meant.

The people arranging the funeral called out, “Come see him one last time.”

Because he had been kept frozen, and because he was not wearing his dentures, his face was thin and pale. He looked nothing like the person I remembered. His burial clothes were like a quilt, tied around him with rope. I could not see his hands. I said a few things to him that I had thought about beforehand, mostly telling him not to worry. Only then did I begin to cry.

It was strange, really. The decisive moment had already happened when the accident occurred. Everything now, all these rituals, could only derive their meaning from believing in a world after death. Maizi had already cried before we arrived. He had come early to No. 95 Hospital and changed out of the brown suit his father had been wearing when the accident happened, though perhaps by then it could no longer really be taken off. That suit belonged to a younger version of Duidui’s grandfather. As he grew older it no longer fit very well, and he often had to roll up the sleeves. But every time he went out to buy vegetables, he liked to dress properly. He wore square brown-tinted glasses. His legs were a little rheumatic. Sometimes he said going to the market was also a way to exercise. Other times he said that when Duidui was not home, he would rather not go out at all.

On the 21st I left Putian. At the time I had been staying on the seventh floor of the same building. We had spent a carefree week there. I sometimes think that if I had stayed longer, perhaps he would not have wanted to go out so early to buy vegetables. Sister Ren said her parents-in-law were the same: rain or shine, even if there was food at home, they still went out early to buy something good for the children.

When I went to Xiaotang Market and looked at the snow peas, I remembered how he would explain at the dinner table that you had to choose them slowly at the supermarket, the tender ones whose peas inside were still flat. A few times I said I wanted braised lamb, and he went to the market to buy it. He told me that the raw lamb was put into a heated vat and wrapped in quilts until it cooked through. I accepted all this goodness as a matter of course. He himself was extremely frugal. Most of what he bought was for Duidui and me.

When I went to buy white clothes for his funeral, I walked down the street and looked at things in the shops, thinking again and again: I should have bought this for him then. I imagined what it would have been like to hand those things to him. Sunlight still fell on the gray city streets. Everything was too late.

Once he told me about the old men and women at Nanmen Market who worked as urban-management officers, driving away people who set up vegetable stalls. I asked about the details and learned that when Nanmen’s collectively owned land was converted to state ownership, a shareholding company was created. The company used the land to build a market, a hotel, and other facilities, which became its source of income. That was why people from Nanmen were so active in those affairs. He also told me that Maizi’s mother was from Nanmen and had sold her shares to buy a house. He regretted it a little because now the shares could not be bought back, and their value rose soon after she sold them. For the first time, I comforted him and agreed that it would have been better if she had kept them.

His phone was full of pictures of Duidui and some pictures of potted plants. The pictures of Duidui were all extremely cute, and the fondness in them felt almost exactly like my own when I looked at her. The plants were probably from exhibitions Maizi had taken him to. A few days before he died, sometime after four in the afternoon, he took several pictures of goldfish in a tank. Their tails were soft and fragile as they drifted through the water. Maizi said he had saved all those pictures. That was all he could do.

I had known Duidui’s grandfather for about ten years. To know someone for ten years and accept all his goodness so naturally, and then have him suddenly die, felt as though some enormous barrier had been violently pulled away. A fragile middle-aged person, I thought. Then I cried the whole way on the high-speed train.

Duidui told me Grandpa had died during a WeChat video call. She had changed his WeChat account to her own name and picture, and I had not noticed. I asked if she had finally saved enough money to buy a phone. She said it was Grandpa’s phone. I asked whether Grandpa had treated her even better than her father and I did, and whether she was even more unrestrained with him than she was with us. In the video I could see the door of the room. I assumed Grandpa was, as usual, busy in the kitchen. Then Midui said, “Grandpa was in an accident yesterday and died. You didn’t know?”

At that moment, the image I had of him moving around in the kitchen or in the room disappeared. I was so shocked that I would rather have believed Midui was making the cruelest joke imaginable than believe it had really happened.

For years, when I video-called Duidui, Grandpa would sometimes come in carrying fruit for her, cut into little pieces. “Midui, Midui,” he would call in Mandarin with his Putian accent. “You know?” He would add that phrase after a sentence. The ma was not pronounced with standard Mandarin’s first tone, but more like the way it sounds in Chaoshan speech.

I choked up and panicked. I asked, “Then Dad must be very sad, right?” She cheerfully turned the camera toward her father. “Dad is completely speechless.” I said, “Duidui, Mommy is really sad.” Duidui ended the video. I called Maizi and asked if what she had said was true. He said yes. He choked up too, sighing to keep himself from crying. Only then did I realize how indifferent daylight could be. I crouched by the balcony door and sobbed. And in that moment I completely lost the ability to imagine him walking around the kitchen or the room.

I remembered that on the 21st, when I went for a COVID test, I had been only one road away from him. I looked at that familiar window. After the test I simply walked away. I should have bought some fruit, gone over, sat with him for a while, and talked. Since divorcing Maizi, I had not seen him in a long time.

Maizi’s aunt said the “first seven” meant the seventh day after cremation. Midui had been staying with me and had not gone home. I vaguely felt that when her grandfather returned, it would be a pity if he could not see her, but I did not say anything. That day Midui and I went to the library. On the way back we could not get a car, so we decided to take the bus. I told Duidui we needed the No. 21 bus. My phone was almost dead, which made me anxious, and I was so hungry I felt faint.

Duidui said, “The 21 is here!” Without looking up, I boarded and scanned my fare code. The bus drove for a long time. Duidui fell asleep. Half awake, I heard that we had passed No. 95 Hospital, and then we kept going up into the hills. At the second-to-last stop, the man who had jokingly asked us when we boarded, “Which is bigger, one or two?” was getting off. I looked up and said goodbye, thanking him for asking us questions. He asked, “Are you going all the way to the terminal?” Only then did I realize we were on the wrong bus.

No. 95 Hospital was the last place where I saw Duidui’s grandfather. Taking the wrong bus this time made me feel as though, somehow, I was meant to travel that road once more.

At the crematorium there was a place for burning paper money. Turn around, and there was the enormous furnace. Before Duidui’s grandfather was pushed inside, the staff arranged us into several rows and had us bow. I held flowers in my hands. Duidui drew many pictures in her notebook. While we waited for the cremation, more hearses arrived. We stood by the first furnace and watched them come and go. There was no particular smell I can describe.

Maizi’s younger aunt spoke to me for a while. “I thought my brother would live into his nineties. I never imagined…” She said her brother had been admitted to the best high school in the area, had excellent grades, but because his family background was considered bad he had to attend a worse school, and later he was sent down to the countryside. “He suffered a lot. My brother worried about you the most. He loved Midui the most.”

I started crying. I really did owe him too much. She said, “You were young. You didn’t understand. He was very stubborn, but his heart was good.”

Today is April 1, also the anniversary of my own father’s death. When I learned that Duidui’s grandfather had died, I called my mother and cried for a long time, saying how pitiful it was, how much I regretted it. My mother told me many stories she had known but never told us before. Do you remember so-and-so, she asked, who died in a traffic accident when his children were still so young? And one of your father’s old classmates, a retired teacher earning five or six thousand a month, who came to sit with your father and said he could still teach and invited your father to go teach too. Your father had been out of work for years, but because this old friend asked him, he really went back to teaching. Then one weekend that friend was riding his bicycle to school for a meeting and was hit by a car. “That was even more pitiful. When he came to our house, I happened to see him once. Such a gentle person.” His son worshipped him right there by the roadside. It broke your heart to see. Not long afterward, your father became ill and died too.

Death stops everything. Everything becomes impossible to undo. I searched through videos in Baidu Cloud. In one, he is holding a flashlight while Duidui dances around a room with the curtains closed. She darts back and forth, and he laughs as he shines the light on her. In another picture, someone was photographing Duidui and accidentally caught him looking at her with a quiet, delighted smile. He was wearing that old pajama top that never quite fit. He had worn it for years. It never occurred to me to buy him a new one. Thinking about it makes me very sad.

Life continues. I am spending down my savings, maintaining the appearance of life much as before. From the outside, not much seems to have changed. But I feel that the barrier between me and reality has disappeared. It makes me realize that I had been protected all along without knowing it. I think of his goodness now with even more gratitude than when he was alive. Why did I not stop and think carefully about these things earlier?

A few days ago my mother called to ask for my phone account information. She was hurrying to see my grandmother. “Grandma is going,” she said in a WeChat voice message. Then she thought of my situation and said that the most important thing now was simply to raise the child. What had happened could not be changed. I had already studied for so many years. It was enough. “Don’t come. You just attended a funeral. Don’t give people a reason to resent you,” she told me sternly.

Grandma gestured to her that she was ninety-three and told her not to be sad. My mother talked with her late into the night until my younger aunt told her not to talk so much. “I’m afraid there won’t be another chance.” “She tried to take off her ring for me, and then tried to pull the silver bangle off her wrist.” My mother sent me voice messages on WeChat.

“Grandma is going, ha,” she said, the ha in the second tone of Chaoshan speech. “Don’t come.”

Original 中文

住在这里快一个月了，上周堆堆开始线上上课，作业多得搞不清楚，便决定白天堆堆去麦子那里做作业。没课的时候，我就去楼下的一条石凳坐着抽会烟看书。状态时好时坏，但是《读书与跌宕自喜》我随时打开都能看下去。

其实抽到第三根就觉得冷了，也困。只想快快抽完回去。心里空荡荡的。马雁写她和朋友去郊游，去吃叶鸭子，去书店，而我在这个疫情封锁的菜市场的中间，周围是六七层高的楼，没有太阳的时候，我坐的位置非常冷。有太阳的时候看书抬头看周围都是蒙上一片绿色。在一座城市没有任何朋友，没有任何联结，长久以来均是如此。

生活还照旧。

巷子里有一家卖白事的店，一捆一捆的金元宝。夜晚店里的灯也不亮，店主几人坐在店门口看着小巷的路人，店门前是一个低矮的棚子。我和堆堆说，给爷爷买东西就只能买这些了，我们现在不能给他买其他东西了。堆堆想了想，戴口罩的两只小眼睛看着前面的路。“我们还可以给太奶奶买。”我一惊。难道太奶奶怎么了。她纠正说，可以给太奶奶买其他礼物，“但是我不知道她的生日。”心意有的话不需要生日啊。我很刻意地说。太奶奶在我结婚的时候，送给我一个“海洋之心”的蓝色宝石，我自己确实还没有买过礼物给她。

我们住在这六楼，米堆和我都不喜欢爬楼梯。楼梯内的油漆味很重，我平时上楼只管爬楼，脚酸得要命，7楼处有一盏灯是常亮着的，昏黄的灯光投射在五楼半的墙上，就这样知道自己马上要到六楼了。

门口系着红布，堆堆的爷爷在三月初的早晨去买菜，就再也见不到了。葬礼的那天，在九五医院的太平间，见了他最后一面，九五医院的保安指着太平间的方向，道路宽敞，春天的阳光和煦而宁静。树木稀疏的样子，阳光照着四周仿佛有一层薄雾。保安说那边阴气很重，孩子最好不要去吧。爷爷在那里，应该没事吧。太平间是一层的平房，放着一个平台摆放一些祭品，麦子在烧一些金元宝，四周是纸片焚烧的味道。麦子的爷爷、二姨和姑姑和伯伯穿着黑色的衣服站在路边，他们的背后是低矮的围墙，墙上长着荒草，再过去是一小片自留地。亲戚们过来摸摸米堆的头，米堆戴着口罩。笔记本里写着“爷爷跟着走”，是要冥车过桥或者十字路口的时候念。

太平间门口有一只黑色的幼犬，圆圆的脸，在脏污的楼梯爬上爬下。堆堆就在停尸房门口看着狗狗，发出“好可爱”的赞叹声。几年前，外甥女晗晗在她奶奶的葬礼上玩摆在奶奶棺材边上的米。那一刻我只是呆滞。也不知道这一切意味着什么。

安排葬礼的人员喊，来看最后一面吧。

因为冷冻的缘故，也没有戴假牙，脸是瘦削苍白的。一点都不像印象中的样子。寿衣像是一床被子那样，用绳子绑在身上。手也看不见。我对着他说了几句我之前想好的话，大意是希望他放心。那一刻我才哭起来。其实真的奇怪啊，因为决定性的瞬间在车祸发生的时候，此刻的一切，这些仪式，其意义就只能寄托在对死后世界的相信之中。麦子在见我们之前已经哭过，他早早就到九五医院，将车祸发生时穿着的褐色西装换下来，但是可能已经换不下来了吧。那件西装是米堆爷爷更年轻时穿的，因为上了年纪，渐渐已经不合身了，袖子也常常要卷起来。但是他每次去买菜都要穿得很体面。戴着茶色的方框眼镜，腿脚有点风湿，于是他说有时候去买菜是顺便锻炼，但是有时候也说堆堆不在家，他就宁愿不要出门的。

21号的时候，我离开莆田，当时住在同一栋楼的7楼。我们无忧无虑地度过一个星期。也会想，如果我多待一段时间，或许他就不会想早早出门买菜了。韧姐说，她的家公家婆也是，风雨无阻，家里有菜也要早早出门，给孩子买点好的。我去筱塘市场买菜，看着那些荷兰豆，想起他常常在饭桌上说，如何在超市里面慢慢挑，一定要挑嫩嫩的，中间的籽还长得扁扁的那一种。我有几次说要吃焖羊肉，他就去菜市场买。他告诉我，那是生羊肉放进一个烧热的大缸，用棉被包起来焖熟。其实我也心安理得地接受这一切的好，他自己是省吃俭用的，大部分也是我和堆堆吃。

我去买白色的衣服参加他的葬礼的时候，走在街上看到店铺里的很多东西，都想，要是当时我买给他就好了。并想象把东西拿给他的样子。日光照旧照着灰蒙蒙的城市街道，一切都太迟了。

有一次他讲南门市场的老头老太在那里做城管，赶走那些摆摊卖菜的，我问起细节来，知道南门集体土地国有化的时候，做成一个股份公司，公司拿了地开了市场、酒店和其他一些设施，这是公司的收入来源，所以南门人做这些事情是积极的。又讲起麦子的母亲是南门人，当时为了买房卖掉股份，他有点可惜，因为现在想买也不行，而且卖掉不久就涨价了。我第一次安慰他，并同意他说要是没卖掉就好了。

他的手机里是堆堆的照片和一些盆栽的照片，堆堆的照片都是十分可爱的，和我看到的堆堆时候的喜欢几乎是一样的。盆栽大概是麦子带他去看的一些盆栽展览拍的。在去世的前几天，下午四点多，手机里拍着几张鱼缸里的金鱼，金鱼的尾巴柔软而脆弱，在鱼缸里悠悠地游着。麦子说他把这些照片都存起来了。他能做的就只有这些了。

和堆堆的爷爷认识，算起来是有十年的光景。认识一个人十年，而心安理得地接受一切的好，所以当他突然离世，我觉得巨大的屏障被猛然抽掉了。脆弱的中年人，我想，然后高铁上哭了一路。

堆堆告诉我爷爷已经去世了，是在一个微信电话，她用爷爷的微信换成自己的名字和头像，我没发觉，我说你是不是存够了钱买了手机。她说那是爷爷的手机。我说爷爷对你是不是比我和爸爸对你还要好，你对爷爷是不是比对我们还要更肆无忌惮啊……米堆的视频里可以看到房间的门，我以为爷爷像往常一样，正在厨房里忙。米堆说，爷爷昨天出了车祸，已经去世了你不知道吗？那一刻我想象中的他在厨房或者在房间里走动的形象消失了，惊愕地宁愿相信这是米堆和我开的一个最恶毒的玩笑也不愿相信这件事情已经发生。

几年来我和堆堆视频的时候，爷爷偶尔会进来，给堆堆端个水果，水果切成一小块一小块的，“米堆米堆”，他用莆田口音的普通话喊米堆，“你知道吗？”他说完一句话会加上这句口头禅，吗的发音不是普通话的一声，而是更接近潮汕话的一声。

我哽咽，慌乱，问那爸爸一定很难过吧？她笑嘻嘻地拿镜头对着她爸爸，“爸爸都无语了”。我说，堆堆，妈妈真的很难过，堆堆把视频切断了。我打电话给麦子，问堆堆说的是真的吗？麦子说是，他也哽咽了，叹气不让自己哭出来，我那时候才意识到日光多么冷漠，我蹲在阳台的门边呜咽地哭起来。而我在那一刻，就彻底失去任何他在厨房和在房间走动的想象。

想起来我21号那天去测核酸，和他就离一条马路的距离，我看着那个熟悉的窗户，做完核酸又走了。我应该买点水果去和他坐一坐，聊一聊。自从和麦子离婚，我已经好久没有见他了。

麦子的姑姑说，头七是指火化后的第七天。米堆一直在我那里住，没有回去家里，我隐约觉得她爷爷回来的时候看不到她会遗憾，但也没说什么。那一天我和米堆去图书馆，回去的时候叫不到车，就打算坐公交回去。我和堆堆说，我们要坐21路，手机快没电了我有点焦虑，肚子饿地心慌。堆堆说“21路到了！”我便头也不抬地上车刷码。车一直开了很久，堆堆也睡着了，迷糊中听到车经过了九五医院，便一路往山上去了。到了倒数第二个站，那位我们上车时候戏问我们“一和二哪一个更大”的大叔要下车，我抬头和他打招呼，谢谢他问我们问题，他说你们要去终点站吗？那时候才知道坐错了车。

As a child I thought there were two possibilities: either rocks like that really existed, or the two of them had actually died.

The bookstore was beautiful. Even though I suppose I can already count myself as someone who reads, I still felt an involuntary pressure when I entered. Ah, I don’t belong here. I lost confidence.

We read A Doll’s House, Desire Under the Elms, Uncle Vanya, La Dame aux Camélias, and Su Fang’s Notes from a Foreign Place. The host, Ruan Shui, had read every story carefully. During each group’s performance she narrated and stepped into whatever roles no one else was playing.

Naturally, we began talking about marriage. I wondered whether the institution of marriage is inherently disadvantageous to women. Certain unspoken traditions keep making demands of women, demands that are never stated clearly before marriage. Can a woman choose whether or not to have children? Perhaps she can. But the immediate consequence may be the collapse of the marriage, which seems to suggest that bearing children is something the institution of marriage was designed to require of women.

I have no answer. My feeling has become a little cynical. Perhaps that is my problem.

I imagine the good part of marriage is that two people have a small world of their own, and having each other is enough. The outside world becomes less important. One’s inner life can be cared for, and that kind of feeling is what makes something last. Marriage can be the form, but it can also be something else. The standardized shell of marriage is far too deceptive. Su Fang’s Notes from a Foreign Place writes about women, but also about men. Even when the men are terrible, even when I cannot accept them, somehow I can still understand them a little. Perhaps a writer has to be capable of that. I do not have that breadth of spirit.

I had not visited my grandmother for two years. On Friday, my mother and I went to Jilong. My mother has three younger brothers, and they all live fairly close to one another. Using the location my cousin sent me, I first went to my eldest uncle’s home. I used to come there often. As soon as I got out of the car and saw the utility pole at the corner, I remembered waiting there with the makeup artist when my cousin got married. I naturally walked toward the back of the main street. The storefronts downstairs had all been rented out, so the entrance to the residence was now at the back. In the end, my eldest uncle still came out to get us. He took my mother and me on his cruiser-style motorcycle to my second uncle’s home, and the next day to my youngest uncle’s. I am someone who listens carefully when other people speak. Small talk is exhausting for me.

Jilong specializes in making leather shoes, as opposed to athletic shoes. Everywhere there are shops selling leather and accessories and factories making insoles. My youngest aunt-by-marriage sat with us in her office drinking tea. Every so often a worker would bring in a shoe. When there was a defect in an insole, glue, or assembly, she would inspect it, explain the problem to the supplier of the component, offer a solution, and communicate with the person who placed the order about color differences or style issues. She rode out on her motorcycle and came back a little later with two rolls of leather. There was nothing on the partition wall in the office except a calendar printed in gold: “Huangbu Chaoshan Chamber of Commerce.” Their family lived on the top floor of a four-story building. The second and third floors were production workshops. A monitor in the office showed six surveillance-camera views. The stairwell was stacked with boxes of shoes.

My youngest uncle was very lively in front of his older sister. When he heard that my mother’s lower back hurt, he lay on a bench and demonstrated how sleeping on one’s stomach with a pillow under the thighs could correct it. He was not embarrassed in the slightest. He spoke very loudly and cared a great deal about loyalty. Then he said that society was full of people who forgot loyalty when profit appeared, and that everyone only looked at how useful you were to them.

Grandma said she had lived fifty years of misery. She was ninety-two.

She counted those fifty years from the marriage arranged by her parents, when she entered my grandfather’s household. Twenty loads of rice were exchanged for a silver phoenix crown plated with gold. She walked to Kengzai Village and cried the whole way. “He and his mother called me bairen,” she said. Bairen means a fool, and is insulting. “At first I didn’t answer back. Later I said: in my village, Lanbiao, everyone treated me like a ‘red person,’ someone important. I came here and you made my life bitter.” She did all the housework. The middle parts of the sweet potatoes went to her husband and mother-in-law. She ate the ends, placed at the bottom of the pot, the parts that burned.

Nine days after she married into the family, the household was classified as landlords because my grandfather’s father, after gambling debts, had been sold overseas to Taiwan as a hired laborer. Although the family was rehabilitated more than ten years later, at the time of the landlord classification half of their traditional xiashanhu house was partitioned off and assigned to a poor peasant. That person stayed for fifty years. Only later did the family pay to buy it back.

After collectivization began, my grandmother was not allowed to come home from the fields to care for my young mother and uncles. Her mother-in-law died at fifty-nine. After she died, my mother, then two years old, had no one to look after her. She could only sit on the floor and cry for an entire day, with no one to clean up her urine or feces. My mother said that was how the skin on her heels was worn away against the floor.

I asked my mother why Grandma could not occasionally come back from the fields to check on the children. She said that doing so would cost work points, and then there might not be enough food to eat. After the household responsibility system began in 1983, my mother herself raised four children. By the time the older girls came home from school, she would already have cooked. The older children looked after the younger ones who were not yet in school while she hurried to the fields. She finished her work, came back to eat something, then when the older sister returned to school, she put the little one to sleep and hurried back to the fields. During the slack farming season she could sometimes stay home.

The communes promised a utopia of kindergartens and nurseries. In reality, children like my mother, who had no one to care for them, simply cried at home all day. I had nothing to say after hearing this story.

两年没有去探望外婆了，我母亲和我在周五去了一趟吉隆。我母亲有三个弟弟，住处都相距不远。我通过表姐提供的定位，先去我大舅家。大舅家是我过去常常来的，我一下车，看到拐角电线杆的时候，想到我表姐结婚的时候，我们和化妆师在电线杆那里等车，自然走到主街背面，楼下店面都出租了，所以住宅入口安在了背面。后来还是大舅来接了。我大舅用他的太子摩托，带着我们去二舅家，第二天又带我们去小舅家。我是一个仔细听别人讲话的人，闲聊对我来说很累，

吉隆是专门生产皮质鞋的地方（区别于生产运动鞋），到处都是卖皮料和配件、生产鞋垫的地方。小舅妈坐在办公室和我们喝茶，一会儿工人拿着鞋过来，遇到一些鞋垫瑕疵、黏胶、组装问题，她看了问题之后，和生产配件的人说出问题所在，提供解决方案，和下单人沟通色差或款式问题。她骑着摩托车出去，一会儿带回来两卷皮料。办公室隔间的墙上什么都没有，就一个日历，写着金色的字“黄埠潮汕商会”。他们一家住在四层楼的顶层，第二层和第三层是生产车间，办公室的液晶显示屏上是六格画面的监控。楼道里是一箱一箱的鞋子。

小舅在他姐姐面前非常活泼，听说我母亲腰痛，他在长椅上演示如何通过趴着睡在大腿处垫枕头的方式矫正。他没有一点不好意思。他说话十分大声，十分讲江湖义气，又说这个社会都是见利忘义，别人都只看你的利用价值。

外婆说，她度过了50年的悲惨生活。她今年92岁。

五十年，从父母主婚，嫁进外公家门开始计算，20担稻子，换了一头镀了金的银制凤冠，走路去坑仔村，哭了一路。“他和他母亲，喊我白仁，”（白仁是傻子的意思，带有侮辱性）“刚开始我不反驳，到了后来我说，我在我览表村，个个当我是‘红人’，我是到你这里被苦成这样的。”做所有家务活，番薯的中间给丈夫和婆婆吃，自己吃番薯尾，番薯尾房在锅底，糊掉的部分。

进门九天，被划为地主，因为我外公的父亲因为赌债被卖到番外（台）做长工。虽然十几年后平反了，但当时被划地主之后，把下山虎从厅划开另一边分给了贫农，这个人后来一直不走，住了50年。后来才花钱买回来。

集体化开始之后，我外婆必须不能回家照顾年幼的我母亲和舅舅，外婆的婆婆59岁就死了，她死后我母亲两岁没人带，只能一个人坐在地板上哭上一天，屎尿没人收拾，我母亲说她的脚后跟就那样在地板上磨没的。

我问我母亲，为什么不能偶尔从田间回来看孩子，答这样会扣工分，就可能没饭吃。83年承包制之后，我母亲自己也带着四个孩子，姐姐们放学来，她已经做好饭，小的还没上学的让大的带，她匆匆赶到田里，做完回来吃点饭，姐姐去上学她哄小的睡着，又赶到田里，农闲时也可以不去田里——

公社化承诺了有幼儿园、托儿所的乌托邦，可实际上就是让如我母亲那样没人带的孩子，再家里哭一天。我听这个故事说不出话来。
